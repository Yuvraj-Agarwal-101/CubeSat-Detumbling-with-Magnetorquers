\section{Engineering results}
\subsection{Measured study and data quality}
The final experiment contains 16 real, targeted engineering cases, not an
exhaustive parameter sweep. Fifteen met the recovery criterion and one was
right-censored at the six-orbit deadline; there were no invalid simulations.
Thus 15/16 (93.75\%) is the observed fraction for this deliberately selected
design, not an estimated population success probability. All histories end
at $34008.8662\ \mathrm{s}$ and contain 136037 samples, using the accepted
$0.25\ \mathrm{s}$ RK4 step with a shortened final step.
Success requires a rate norm at or below $0.5\ \mathrm{deg/s}$ continuously
for 600 seconds, with the complete hold ending by the deadline.

Among successful cases, recovery times range from $862.94$ to
$20622.34\ \mathrm{s}$, with conditional median $8043.23\ \mathrm{s}$.
The censored case has no reported recovery time. Saturation fractions span
0 to 0.484305, and output-sampled peak torques span
$4.2844\times10^{-8}$ to $8.5728\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{N\,m}$.
Independent checks account for every intended case, confirm finite arrays,
recompute every metric, and verify the command norm bound and full deadline.
Maximum output quaternion norm error is $3.33\times10^{-16}$;
the largest preprojection norm defect is $1.28\times10^{-8}$.
Projection controls the constraint and is not itself an accuracy proof.
The existing Stage 3 refinement evidence supplies the numerical basis for
the accepted step; no further refinement integrations were added to this
deadline-limited targeted experiment.

\subsection{Initial spin and normalized B-dot gain}
At geometry 0 and limit $0.03\ \mathrm{A\,m^2}$, increasing initial spin
from 1 to $30\ \mathrm{deg/s}$ at gain $3\times10^{-6}$ changes the outcome
from recovery at $9491.87\ \mathrm{s}$ to a censored deadline failure.
The latter ends at $4.4130\ \mathrm{deg/s}$. With gain
$3\times10^{-5}$, both spins recover, but the recovery time increases
from $862.94$ to $20622.34\ \mathrm{s}$. Higher initial spin therefore makes
recovery harder in these matched tests.

At $1\ \mathrm{deg/s}$, increasing gain by a factor of ten reduces recovery
time from $9491.87$ to $862.94\ \mathrm{s}$, a 90.91\% reduction. Neither
trajectory saturates. At $30\ \mathrm{deg/s}$ and the same actuator limit,
the gain increase changes a censored outcome to success, while saturation
increases from 7.67\% to 48.43\%. The tested gain change has a substantial
effect, but these two gain levels do not establish an optimal gain or a
global monotonic trend.

\subsection{Actuator capability and saturation}
For $30\ \mathrm{deg/s}$, gain $3\times10^{-5}$, and geometry 0, increasing
the limit from 0.03 to $0.3\ \mathrm{A\,m^2}$ reduces recovery time from
$20622.34$ to $8043.23\ \mathrm{s}$ (61.00\%). Saturation falls from
48.43\% to 1.56\%; peak torque increases from 1.337 to
$8.573\ \mu\mathrm{N\,m}$. This matched comparison shows that actuator
clipping materially restricts the high-spin, higher-gain trajectory.

In contrast, at $1\ \mathrm{deg/s}$ and gain $3\times10^{-6}$, all three
limits are inactive for all four geometries. Recovery times and trajectories
are identical across limits at a fixed geometry. Raising capability above
$0.03\ \mathrm{A\,m^2}$ offers no observed benefit in those tested cases.
This is an inactive-bound plateau, not evidence that larger actuators are
unnecessary at other spins or gains.

\subsection{Geometry and computational cost}
At $1\ \mathrm{deg/s}$ and gain $3\times10^{-6}$, geometries 0--3 recover
at $9491.87$, $2448.32$, $9529.17$, and $5663.47\ \mathrm{s}$ respectively.
The slowest is 3.89 times the fastest. Each geometry jointly varies initial
attitude, spin direction, and orbital phase, so these results demonstrate
combined geometry sensitivity rather than isolating the effect of one
orientation variable. Four samples do not characterize a distribution.

The first twelve cases took 401.84--407.32 seconds each under 12-worker
load, completing in 408.26 seconds wall time. The final four took
239.65--240.56 seconds each under four-worker load, completing in
241.21 seconds wall time. Different concurrency prevents treating those
case times as a controller-performance difference. No further engineering
simulations, full pilot, or production sweep were run after the final batch.
